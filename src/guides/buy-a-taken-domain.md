Most good names are registered. That's not the end of the road: names change hands every day, from four-figure deals for small businesses to eight-figure deals for global brands. Buying a taken domain is a negotiation like any other, with a few domain-specific rules that protect your money.

## Step 1: Confirm what you're dealing with

Run the name through our [domain lookup](../../tools/domain-lookup/). Note:

- **Expiry date and status.** If the status shows *redemption period* or *pending delete*, the owner may be letting it go. You might be able to catch it when it drops instead of buying it. See [what happens when a domain expires](../domain-expiry-lifecycle/).
- **Nameservers.** Parking or marketplace nameservers usually mean the name is listed for sale. Nameservers from a major host usually mean an active site.
- **Creation date.** Very old registrations often belong to owners who value them highly.

Then visit the domain. An active business is the hardest seller: the name is part of its identity. A parked page with ads or a for-sale banner is the easiest.

## Step 2: Decide your ceiling before you talk to anyone

Write down the most you'll pay and why. Useful anchors:

- **What the name is worth to you:** the cost of the alternative name plus the extra marketing it would need.
- **Comparable sales:** public sales reports list what similar names sold for. Look for the same extension, length and type of word.
- **Our [domain score](../../tools/domain-score/)** to sanity-check how the market might see the name.

A ceiling stops you from bidding against yourself in the excitement of a deal. Our [deal calculator](../../tools/deal-calculator/) shows what you'll pay in total once escrow or broker fees are added.

## Step 3: Choose a route

| Route | Best when | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Buy-now listing | The name is listed with a price | The price, plus or including escrow |
| Direct negotiation | You can reach the owner and stay anonymous | Escrow fee only |
| Buy-side broker | Your identity would raise the price, or the owner is hard to reach | A fee and/or commission on top |
| Backorder | The name is expiring | A backorder fee, and an auction if others want it too |

A large registrar's broker service, for example, lists a flat fee of $99.99 plus a 20% commission on the purchase price, with no guarantee of success. Independent brokers vary; ask for the fee structure in writing.

## Step 4: The first message

Keep it short and neutral:

> Subject: example.com
>
> Hi, I'm interested in example.com. Would you consider an offer for it?
> Thanks, Sam

Don't name a price. Don't mention your company or your launch date. If the owner replies with a price, you have learned their anchor without giving yours. If they ask for an offer, start well below your target and leave room for two or three moves.

Use a personal or neutral email address if your company name would signal deep pockets.

## Step 5: Negotiate

- **Move in shrinking steps.** Big jumps signal there is a lot more room.
- **Ask questions.** "What price did you have in mind?" and "Is there a number that would work this week?" reveal more than counteroffers.
- **Offer terms, not just money.** A faster close, payment in installments, or covering all fees can bridge a gap.
- **Be ready to walk away** at your ceiling. Many deals close weeks after a buyer says "that's beyond my budget", when the seller comes back.

## Step 6: Close through escrow

Never send money directly to a seller you don't know, and never let a seller transfer the name before money is secured. A licensed escrow service holds the payment until the domain is in your account:

1. Buyer or seller opens a transaction with the agreed price and who pays the fee.
2. Buyer pays the escrow service.
3. The escrow service confirms funds; the seller starts the transfer.
4. Buyer confirms receipt of the domain; escrow releases payment.

Escrow fees for domains are typically a few percent at lower prices, with a minimum fee, and fall as a percentage for larger amounts. The largest escrow provider's domain fees start at 2.6% with a $50 minimum.

## Step 7: The transfer

There are two ways a domain moves:

- **Push within the same registrar.** If you have an account at the seller's registrar, the seller moves the name to your account. It takes minutes.
- **Inter-registrar transfer.** The seller unlocks the domain and gives you an authorization (EPP) code; you start the transfer at your registrar. It usually completes within five days.

Watch for **transfer locks**. Many registrars block transfers to another registrar for 60 days after a registration or a change of owner. If the seller recently bought the name, plan for a push or a delay.

## Step 8: After you own it

- Change the account email, password and two-factor settings if the account came with the name.
- Renew for several years and turn on auto-renew.
- Turn on the transfer lock, and ask about registry lock if the name is critical.
- Point DNS where you need it, and add the name to your [Desk](../../desk/) watchlist.

## Red flags

- A seller who insists on wire transfer or crypto outside escrow.
- An "escrow service" you have never heard of, suggested by the seller. Type the escrow provider's address yourself.
- A seller who can't show control of the domain, for example by adding a TXT record you specify.
- Pressure to close today.
