A good name is easy to say, easy to spell, means nothing bad anywhere you sell, and comes with a domain you can actually get. Most naming projects fail on the last two. This process gets you to a name that passes all four.

## 1. Write a one-page brief

Before brainstorming, answer five questions:

1. **What do you do,** in one sentence a customer would use?
2. **Who is it for,** and where do they live?
3. **What should the name feel like?** Pick three adjectives: trustworthy, playful, premium, technical, local.
4. **What must it avoid?** Competitor names, words with baggage, hard-to-spell sounds.
5. **What's your domain budget?** A hand-registered name costs a registration fee; an aftermarket .com can cost from a few hundred to many thousands of dollars.

The brief keeps the team honest when a clever but wrong name shows up.

## 2. Choose a name style

| Style | Example pattern | Strengths | Watch-outs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | Toronto Roofing Co. | Instantly clear | Hard to trademark, hard to stand out |
| Suggestive | A word that hints at a benefit | Memorable and protectable | Needs a little explaining |
| Compound | Two short words joined | Often available on .com | Can get long |
| Invented | A new word built from sounds | Very protectable, often available | Needs marketing to give it meaning |
| Metaphor | A word from another world | Distinctive story | Popular metaphors are taken |
| Founder or place | A name or landmark | Personal, authentic | Harder to sell the business later |

Suggestive, compound and invented names usually give the best mix of memorability, legal protection and domain availability.

## 3. Generate a long list

Aim for 100 or more raw ideas before judging any. Combine:

- Words from your brief, synonyms, and words from the world you work in.
- Our [name generator](../../tools/name-generator/), which builds compounds, blends, prefixes, suffixes and invented words and checks each domain as you go.
- A short team brainstorm with a timer and no criticism.

## 4. Cut to a shortlist

Score each candidate quickly. Drop any that fail one of these:

- **The radio test.** Say it once to someone. Can they spell it?
- **Length.** Long names get shortened by customers in ways you don't control.
- **Pronunciation.** If there are two ways to say it, people will use both.
- **Meaning.** Search the word in the languages of your main markets.
- **Fit to the brief.** Does it feel like your three adjectives?

Our [domain score](../../tools/domain-score/) helps rank the survivors.

## 5. Screen for trademarks

A name you can't protect, or that infringes someone else's, is an expensive mistake. Search the official databases for your main markets:

- **United States:** USPTO trademark search
- **Canada:** the Canadian Trademarks Database (CIPO)
- **European Union:** EUIPO's database, and TMview for many national offices

Search in the classes of goods and services you'll use (the international Nice classification). An identical or very similar mark in the same or a related class is a red flag. For your final choice, a short consultation with a trademark lawyer is money well spent.

## 6. Secure the domain

Run your shortlist through the [bulk checker](../../tools/bulk-checker/). Then:

- **If the .com is free,** register it today. Good names are registered daily.
- **If the .com is taken but unused,** it may be for sale. See [how to buy a domain someone else owns](../buy-a-taken-domain/).
- **If it's taken and in use,** consider a clear alternative: a strong country code for a local business, a relevant extension, or a short modifier like *get*, *try* or *hq*. Avoid hyphens and numbers.
- **Register the obvious variations** you'd hate a competitor or phisher to own: the main typo and your local country code.

## 7. Check the rest

- **Social handles:** nice to match, not essential.
- **History:** look the domain up in the Wayback Machine.
- **App stores and marketplaces:** search for the name to avoid confusion with an existing product.

## 8. Test with real people

Show your top three to five people in your audience. Ask what each name makes them think the business does, then ask them to write each name from memory an hour later. The name that's remembered and understood wins.
