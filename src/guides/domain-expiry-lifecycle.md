A domain doesn't vanish the moment it expires. For generic extensions such as .com there is a sequence of stages, each with different rules about who can recover the name and at what cost. Knowing the stages protects your own names and helps you catch names you want.

## The timeline for .com

The exact numbers vary by registrar and extension, but a typical .com moves through these stages:

| Stage | Typical length | Can the owner recover it? | What the public record shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| Active | Until the expiry date | Renew any time | Normal status |
| Auto-renew grace | Up to about 45 days after expiry, set by the registrar | Yes, usually at the normal renewal price | Often unchanged, or parked page |
| Redemption grace | 30 days | Yes, with a restore fee that is often much higher | *redemption period* |
| Pending delete | 5 days | No | *pending delete* |
| Drop | One moment | Anyone can register it | Not found |

From expiry to drop, a .com can take roughly 80 days. Some names never reach the drop: registrars often send valuable expired names to auction during the grace period.

## What owners are promised

ICANN's Expired Registration Recovery Policy sets minimum protections for generic domains. Registrars must send renewal reminders before expiry and shortly after, and for a period after expiry they must interrupt the domain's normal DNS so the owner notices: the website or email stops working and often shows a renewal notice. If your site suddenly shows a registrar's parking page, check your expiry date first.

The renewal and restore prices are set by your registrar and must be published. Check yours before you need them.

## Country-code domains are different

Each country-code registry writes its own rules. Some delete names soon after expiry, some hold them for months, and some have no redemption period at all. If you own a .ca, .in, .uk or other country-code name, read your registry's expiry policy once and put the dates on your calendar.

## How to never lose a domain

1. **Auto-renew on.** Every important name.
2. **A payment card that won't expire soon,** plus a backup card. Expired cards cause more lost domains than theft.
3. **A registrar email you read,** on a different domain from the ones it controls, so a lapsed domain can't take your inbox with it.
4. **Multi-year renewals** for names that earn money.
5. **A second set of eyes.** Add every name to a watchlist, like our [Desk](../../desk/), and export the renewal reminders to your calendar.

## Catching an expiring name

If a name you want is expiring:

- **Watch it.** Add it to the Targets list on your Desk; refreshing it shows status changes.
- **Look for registrar auctions** during the grace period, when many registrars auction expiring names they hold.
- **Place backorders** with drop-catching services before the pending-delete stage. Several services compete to register names at the instant they drop; if more than one person backorders, the name usually goes to auction among them.
- **Be patient.** Owners often renew at the last minute, even in redemption.

## Reading status codes

Our [domain lookup](../../tools/domain-lookup/) shows status codes from the registry. The ones that matter here:

- `auto renew period`: the registry has renewed the name automatically; the registrar may still delete it within the grace period.
- `redemption period`: deleted by the registrar, recoverable by the last owner for a fee.
- `pending delete`: on its way out; nothing can stop the drop.
- `client hold` or `server hold`: the name is removed from DNS, which can happen for non-payment or abuse.

## For sellers and investors

Every name in a portfolio is a yearly bill. Review names before renewal, not after. A name that has received no inquiries and no use over several renewals is usually better dropped, and an expiry date is a good moment to decide.
