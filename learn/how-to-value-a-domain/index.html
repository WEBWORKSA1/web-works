How to value a domain name

How buyers, sellers and brokers really price domain names: the factors that matter, comparable sales, wholesale versus retail value, automated appraisals and developed-site multiples.

A domain name is worth what one specific buyer will pay for it. That makes valuation part evidence and part judgment. This guide covers the evidence: the features that move prices, how to use comparable sales, and why the same name has two very different prices depending on who is buying.

The features that move price

Buyers and brokers weigh the same handful of factors. Our domain score rates each of them openly.

  1. Extension. .com is still what most people type by default, and .com names sell for the highest prices across the board. Some country codes (.ca, .de, .co.uk) are strong in their own markets, and .io and .ai have active tech-buyer markets.
  2. Length. Shorter is rarer and easier to remember. Prices climb steeply for very short names.
  3. Clarity. One common word beats two; two beat three. Names people can spell after hearing once are worth more.
  4. Commercial intent. Words tied to industries that spend heavily to win a customer (insurance, legal, finance, software, health, travel, real estate) attract bigger budgets.
  5. Clean history. A past as a spam or malware site can hurt email deliverability and search visibility. Check the Wayback Machine.
  6. No trademark conflict. A name that matches someone else's mark carries legal risk, which serious buyers price in, or avoid entirely.

Comparable sales

The strongest evidence is what similar names actually sold for. Public sales are reported in weekly industry sales charts and searchable sales databases. When you look for comparables:

  • Match the extension first, then length, then word type (dictionary word, two-word phrase, brandable invention, acronym, number).
  • Prefer recent sales. Prices for some categories, like short acronyms or AI-related words, change quickly.
  • Remember that reported sales are skewed upwards. Most sales, especially small ones, are never reported.

Wholesale versus retail

The same name has two prices:

  • Wholesale is what another domain investor will pay. Investors buy to resell, so they pay a fraction of what they expect from an end user.
  • Retail is what an end user, a business that wants to use the name, will pay. This is where the large reported sales come from.

The gap can be ten times or more. If you need cash quickly, you'll get wholesale. If you can wait for the right business to come along, you can ask retail, but you may wait years and pay renewals in the meantime.

Liquidity: how fast it sells

A name's value is also about how many buyers want it. A clear, short .com in a busy industry has many potential buyers. An invented word on an unusual extension may have one perfect buyer, or none. Low liquidity doesn't mean low value, but it does mean a longer wait and a lower wholesale price.

Automated appraisals

Appraisal tools give an instant number by comparing the name with past sales and keyword data. They're useful for sorting a big list and for catching obvious mistakes, but treat them as a starting point:

  • They can't know whether a specific buyer needs the name.
  • They often overvalue long keyword phrases and undervalue strong brandables.
  • Different tools can disagree widely on the same name.

Use one as a reference point, then check real comparables.

Developed websites

If the domain comes with a working business, the site is valued on earnings, not just the name. Small content and e-commerce sites are commonly priced as a multiple of average monthly net profit, often somewhere around 25 to 45 times, depending on the site's age, traffic trend, how many income sources it has and how much work it takes to run. A strong domain adds to that, but a weak site on a great name is mostly worth the name.

Putting a number on it

  1. Score the name for the factors above.
  2. Find five to ten comparable sales and note the range.
  3. Decide whether you're pricing for wholesale or retail.
  4. Adjust for liquidity: how many businesses could use this name?
  5. Set a buy-now price a little below the top of your retail range, and a floor you'd accept from a serious offer.

If you're buying, the same work tells you your ceiling. Plug the price into the deal calculator to see your total cost once fees are added.

Common mistakes

  • Valuing a name by what you paid for it. The market doesn't care.
  • Using one big sale as a benchmark. It's the exception, not the rule.
  • Ignoring holding costs. Ten years of renewals while you wait for a retail buyer add up, especially on extensions with high renewal prices.
  • Paying for an appraisal because a "buyer" told you to. This is a common scam: the buyer disappears after you pay the appraiser.
