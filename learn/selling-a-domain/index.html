How to sell a domain name

How to price, list and sell a domain: buy-now versus offers, marketplaces and landing pages, outbound sales, brokers, payment plans, escrow, transfer and the scams to avoid.

Selling a domain is mostly about being findable when the right buyer looks, and being ready to close safely when they do. Here is how to set a price, where to list, how to handle offers and how to get paid.

1. Decide what you're selling

  • A name only: the domain itself.
  • A name with a site: traffic, content or revenue come with it, which changes how it's valued. See how to value a domain name.

Check the name's record with our domain lookup: a buyer will, and they'll notice an expiry date only weeks away or a missing transfer lock.

2. Set a price strategy

Strategy When it works Trade-off
Buy-now price Most names; buyers can act instantly You may leave money on the table for a rare buyer
Make an offer Very strong names with unpredictable buyers Many buyers never make an offer
Buy-now plus minimum offer A middle path Needs a marketplace that supports it
Lease to own Higher prices, buyers who can't pay at once You wait for the full price

Buy-now prices sell more names. A clear price tells a small business it can afford the name; "make an offer" tells them it's probably too expensive.

3. List where buyers look

  • Major marketplaces syndicate listings to registrars' search results, so a buyer searching for your name at their registrar sees it's for sale. That network is where many end-user sales come from.
  • A landing page on the domain itself. Point the domain at a simple page with the price, an offer form and a trust note about escrow. Many marketplaces provide one free.
  • Your own portfolio page if you sell many names.

Commissions vary by marketplace and by channel, commonly 10 to 20%. Use the deal calculator to see your net on each route.

4. Go to buyers directly

For names with obvious end users, outbound sales can work:

  1. List businesses that would benefit: companies using the same words in their name, or ranking for the keyword.
  2. Send a short, personal email: the name, one sentence on why it fits them, and a price or a link to the listing.
  3. Follow up once. Then stop.

Avoid anything that looks like pressure or a threat, especially to a company whose trademark matches the name. That can look like bad-faith registration and lead to a dispute you'd lose.

5. Use a broker for valuable names

A sell-side broker earns a commission, typically higher than a marketplace's, in return for actively pitching the name to qualified buyers and negotiating. Brokers are worth it for names that could fetch five figures or more, or when you don't have time to sell.

6. Handle inbound offers

  • Reply quickly and politely, even to low offers. A lowball today can become a real offer later.
  • Ask what the buyer plans to do with the name. A funded startup and a hobbyist have very different budgets.
  • Counter with your price, and explain it briefly with comparable sales.
  • Offer a payment plan if price is the only obstacle.

7. Close safely

  1. Agree price, who pays fees and the transfer method in writing.
  2. The buyer opens an escrow transaction, or you open it, with a licensed escrow service.
  3. Wait until escrow confirms the funds are secured. Not before.
  4. Transfer the domain: a push to the buyer's account at the same registrar, or unlock it and send the authorization code.
  5. The buyer confirms receipt; escrow pays you.

Marketplaces handle these steps for listed sales.

8. Avoid the common scams

  • The appraisal scam. A "buyer" makes a big offer but insists you get a paid appraisal from a specific company first. The buyer disappears after you pay.
  • Fake escrow sites. Only use an escrow service you found yourself. Type its address; don't click the buyer's link.
  • Transfer before payment. Never.
  • Overpayment. A buyer "accidentally" sends too much and asks for a refund of the difference before their payment bounces.

9. After the sale

Keep records of the sale price and your original cost; in most countries a domain sale is taxable income or a capital gain. Ask an accountant how it applies to you.
